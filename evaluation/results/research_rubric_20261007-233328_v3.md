## rq01: What research gaps exist across these papers and how could they be addressed?
Papers: dpr, rag, fid

**Evidence**
- E1. The FEVER evidence extraction sub-task is not straightforward for the authors because they use a different Wikipedia dump, and they plan to address this in future work. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.18)
- E2. The authors suggest investigating joint pre‑training of the parametric and non‑parametric components from scratch, possibly with a denoising objective, as future work. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.9)
- E3. The paper notes that making the model more efficient when scaling to a large number of support passages and integrating retrieval into an end‑to‑end trainable system are future research directions. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.5)
- E4. Aggregating and combining evidence from multiple passages is not straightforward when using extractive models, highlighting a limitation of current approaches. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.1)
- E5. SQuAD is considered not ideal for open‑domain QA because many questions lack context without the provided paragraph, and it introduces unwanted bias. (Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering, p.4)
- E6. The authors observe that DPR consistently outperforms BM25 on most datasets except SQuAD, indicating a gap in performance on datasets with limited Wikipedia coverage. (Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering, p.5)
- E7. Providing provenance for decisions and updating world knowledge remain open research problems for retrieval‑augmented generation models. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.1)
- E8. The current approach relies on a separate retrieval step before generation, and the authors plan to integrate retrieval into the model to enable end‑to‑end learning. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.5)
- E9. The authors highlight that scaling the number of retrieved passages is challenging and that more efficient methods are needed for large‑scale retrieval‑augmented generation. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.5)
- E10. The discussion notes that hot‑swapping the retrieval index can update the model without retraining, but joint pre‑training of retrieval and generation components is still an open question. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.9)
- E11. The paper points out that dense passage retrieval models are trained only on question‑passage pairs without additional pretraining, leaving open the possibility of further improvements with more extensive pretraining. (Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering, p.2)
- E12. The authors acknowledge that extracting sentences as evidence for FEVER is difficult due to dataset differences, indicating a gap in applying their method to evidence‑extraction tasks. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.18)

**Analysis (inferred)**
- Current retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) pipelines rely on a disjoint retrieval step, making end‑to‑end training difficult and limiting scalability to many passages. [based on E3, E8, E9; high]
- Joint pre‑training of the parametric (generator) and non‑parametric (retriever) components is largely unexplored, especially with objectives that combine denoising and retrieval alignment. [based on E2, E10; medium]
- Evidence extraction for fact‑checking tasks (e.g., FEVER) is hampered by mismatched Wikipedia dumps and the need to select sentence‑level evidence, a problem not solved by existing RAG models. [based on E1, E12; high]
- Dense passage retrieval (DPR) underperforms on datasets like SQuAD that lack sufficient Wikipedia coverage, suggesting a gap in handling domains with limited or noisy context. [based on E5, E6; medium]
- Existing dense retrievers are trained only on question‑passage pairs without broader pre‑training, leaving potential gains from large‑scale language model pre‑training untapped. [based on E11; high]

**Proposed directions (hypotheses)**
- **End‑to‑End Retrieval‑Generation with Differentiable Retrieval**: Integrating retrieval into the generation model would remove the brittle separate step and allow gradients to improve both components jointly, addressing scalability and training inefficiencies noted in multiple papers. *Test:* Train a differentiable retriever (e.g., MIPS with learned indexes) jointly with T5 on NaturalQuestions and evaluate using Exact Match and Retrieval Latency compared to the two‑stage baseline. [based on E3, E8, E9]
- **Joint Pre‑Training of Retriever and Generator with Denoising Objectives**: A unified pre‑training objective that masks text and forces the model to retrieve relevant passages before reconstructing the masked tokens could align the two modules from scratch, as suggested by the authors. *Test:* Pre‑train on a large Wikipedia dump using a masked language modeling + retrieval loss, then fine‑tune on TriviaQA; compare to standard RAG using BLEU and Retrieval Recall. - **Cross‑Dataset Retrieval for Fact‑Checking with Unified Wikipedia Snapshots**: Standardizing the Wikipedia version across training and evaluation would reduce evidence‑extraction mismatches for FEVER and similar tasks, enabling more reliable assessment of RAG models on fact‑checking. *Test:* Re‑run the FEVER evidence extraction sub‑task using a fixed 2023 Wikipedia dump; measure sentence‑level evidence precision/recall and downstream claim verification accuracy against the original setup. [based on E1, E12]
- **Hybrid Retrieval for Low‑Coverage Domains**: Combining dense retrieval with traditional lexical methods (BM25) may compensate for DPR’s weakness on datasets like SQuAD where Wikipedia coverage is sparse, improving overall QA performance. *Test:* Create a hybrid retriever that interpolates DPR scores with BM25 on SQuAD and NaturalQuestions; evaluate top‑k retrieval Recall@100 and downstream QA Exact Match. [based on E5, E6]

## rq02: What are the limitations of these retrieval approaches?
Papers: dpr, colbert

**Evidence**
- E1. Dense retrieval can outperform traditional sparse retrieval, but more complex model frameworks or similarity functions do not necessarily provide additional value. (Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering, p.9)
- E2. Open-domain QA suffers a huge performance degradation when using the standard two-stage retriever‑reader pipeline, indicating the need for improved retrieval. (Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering, p.1)
- E3. The dense retriever is trained on a small number of questions and passages using a simple dual‑encoder framework. (Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering, p.1)
- E4. The approach assumes an extractive QA setting where answers must be spans present in the retrieved passages. (Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering, p.2)
- E5. ColBERT’s late‑interaction operator is designed to enable end‑to‑end retrieval from a large collection, but exhaustive evaluation of all candidates is infeasible when the collection is very large. (ColBERT: Efficient and Effective Passage Search via Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT, p.5)
- E6. ColBERT improves recall relative to term‑based retrieval approaches, yet the initial BM25 top‑k retrieval is required because the official BM25 ranking is not ordered. (ColBERT: Efficient and Effective Passage Search via Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT, p.8)
- E7. BERT‑based retrieval dramatically raises precision (MRR@10) but also increases latency by up to tens of thousands of milliseconds, creating a quality‑cost trade‑off. (ColBERT: Efficient and Effective Passage Search via Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT, p.2)
- E8. Recent NLU‑augmented retrieval methods reduce latency but generally reduce precision substantially relative to BERT‑based approaches. (ColBERT: Efficient and Effective Passage Search via Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT, p.2)

**Analysis (inferred)**
- Dense retrievers rely on limited training data, which may restrict their ability to generalize to diverse query distributions. [based on E3; high]
- Both dense and late‑interaction models assume an extractive QA setting, limiting applicability to generative or abstractive answer tasks. [based on E4, E5; medium]
- ColBERT’s dependence on an initial BM25 top‑k stage introduces a bottleneck: if BM25 fails to retrieve relevant passages, the subsequent dense stage cannot recover them. [based on E6; high]
- BERT‑based retrieval methods achieve higher precision but incur latency orders of magnitude larger than sparse methods, creating a quality‑cost trade‑off that hampers real‑time deployment. [based on E7, E8; high]
- Increasing model complexity (e.g., more sophisticated similarity functions) does not guarantee performance gains, suggesting diminishing returns for architectural enhancements. [based on E1; medium]

**Proposed directions (hypotheses)**
- **Scale‑up Dual‑Encoder Training with Diverse Question Sets**: If dense retrievers are limited by small training sets (E3), expanding the question–passage pairs and using curriculum learning may improve generalization without changing the architecture. *Test:* Train DPR on a 10× larger synthetic QA dataset (e.g., generated via T5) and evaluate on Natural Questions and TriviaQA using Recall@100; compare against the original DPR baseline. [based on E3]
- **Joint Retrieval for Extractive and Generative QA**: Current methods assume answers are spans in passages (E4, E5), which restricts use cases; integrating a generative reader that can synthesize answers from retrieved context may broaden applicability. *Test:* Use the same retriever (DPR or ColBERT) to fetch top‑k passages, then feed them to a seq2seq reader (e.g., T5); measure Exact Match and BLEU on a generative QA benchmark (e.g., ELI5). [based on E4, E5]
- **BM25‑Free Late Interaction Retrieval**: ColBERT’s reliance on BM25 top‑k (E6) creates a recall ceiling; a learned index or approximate nearest‑neighbor search over token embeddings could eliminate this dependency. *Test:* Replace BM25 pre‑filter with FAISS IVF‑PQ over token‑level embeddings; evaluate Recall@100 and latency on MS MARCO passage ranking, comparing against the standard ColBERT pipeline. [based on E5, E6]
- **Latency‑Optimized BERT Retrieval via Knowledge Distillation**: BERT‑based retrieval offers high precision but high latency (E7, E8); distilling the model into a smaller transformer or a hybrid sparse‑dense encoder may retain precision while reducing inference time. *Test:* Distill ColBERT into a 6‑layer MiniLM; benchmark MRR@10 and average query latency on a 1M‑document corpus, comparing against original ColBERT and BM25. [based on E7, E8]

## rq03: How could retrieval-augmented generation be improved based on these papers?
Papers: rag, self_rag

**Evidence**
- E1. SELF-RAG adaptively retrieves passages on-demand using reflection tokens, rather than retrieving a fixed number of passages indiscriminately. (SELF-RAG: LEARNING TO RETRIEVE, GENERATE, AND CRITIQUE THROUGH SELF-REFLECTION, p.1)
- E2. SELF-RAG determines whether retrieval would be helpful for a given generation step and emits a retrieval token to invoke a retriever model. (SELF-RAG: LEARNING TO RETRIEVE, GENERATE, AND CRITIQUE THROUGH SELF-REFLECTION, p.1)
- E3. SELF-RAG also generates critique tokens to assess the quality of its own output and guide further retrieval or generation. (SELF-RAG: LEARNING TO RETRIEVE, GENERATE, AND CRITIQUE THROUGH SELF-REFLECTION, p.1)
- E4. SELF-RAG trains a single arbitrary LM end-to-end to both generate text and reflect on retrieved passages using special tokens. (SELF-RAG: LEARNING TO RETRIEVE, GENERATE, AND CRITIQUE THROUGH SELF-REFLECTION, p.3)
- E5. SELF-RAG outperforms state-of-the-art LLMs and retrieval-augmented models on open-domain QA, reasoning, and fact verification tasks, improving factuality and citation accuracy for long-form generations. (SELF-RAG: LEARNING TO RETRIEVE, GENERATE, AND CRITIQUE THROUGH SELF-REFLECTION, p.1)
- E6. The authors leave exploring instruction-tuning of retrieval systems and joint training of retrieval and LM components for future work. (SELF-RAG: LEARNING TO RETRIEVE, GENERATE, AND CRITIQUE THROUGH SELF-REFLECTION, p.20)
- E7. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) models combine parametric and non‑parametric memory and achieve state‑of‑the‑art results on open‑domain QA. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.9)
- E8. RAG models can hot‑swap the retrieval index to update knowledge without retraining the entire model. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.9)
- E9. Future work suggested includes jointly pre‑training the parametric and non‑parametric components from scratch, possibly with a denoising objective similar to BART. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.9)
- E10. The authors note that off‑the‑shelf retrieval models trained on knowledge‑intensive tasks show limited effectiveness for open‑ended generation such as instruction following. (SELF-RAG: LEARNING TO RETRIEVE, GENERATE, AND CRITIQUE THROUGH SELF-REFLECTION, p.20)
- E11. Performance variance arises from using different Wikipedia dumps for retrieval, as newer entities may be missing from older dumps. (SELF-RAG: LEARNING TO RETRIEVE, GENERATE, AND CRITIQUE THROUGH SELF-REFLECTION, p.20)
- E12. RAG’s stronger grounding in factual knowledge reduces hallucinations and offers more control and interpretability, enabling broader societal benefits. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.10)

**Analysis (inferred)**
- Dynamic, on‑demand retrieval using special tokens (as in SELF‑RAG) can reduce unnecessary context and improve factuality compared to static retrieval of a fixed number of passages. [based on E1, E2; high]
- Self‑generated critique tokens provide an internal feedback loop that can steer additional retrieval or regeneration, suggesting that explicit self‑evaluation is beneficial for long‑form generation. [based on E3, E5; medium]
- Current off‑the‑shelf retrievers, trained on narrow knowledge‑intensive tasks, struggle with open‑ended instruction following, indicating a mismatch between retrieval objectives and generation goals. [based on E10; high]
- The quality of the retrieval index (e.g., recency of Wikipedia dumps) materially affects RAG performance, highlighting the need for continual index updates or robust handling of missing entities. [based on E11; medium]
- Joint pre‑training of the parametric LM and the non‑parametric retriever from scratch is largely unexplored, and could yield tighter integration than current two‑stage training pipelines. [based on E9; medium]

**Proposed directions (hypotheses)**
- **Instruction‑Tuned Retrieval via Retrieval‑Token Supervision**: SELF‑RAG shows that a retrieval token can decide when to fetch, but the retrieval model itself is not instruction‑tuned. Aligning retrieval decisions with downstream instruction following may close the gap identified in E10. *Test:* Fine‑tune a retriever on an instruction‑following dataset (e.g., Alpaca or FLAN) using a retrieval‑token loss; compare against standard off‑the‑shelf retriever on the Open‑Domain QA and instruction‑following benchmarks (e.g., NaturalQuestions + AlpacaEval) using Exact Match and ROUGE‑L. [based on E2, E10]
- **Joint End‑to‑End Pre‑training of LM and Retriever with Denoising Objective**: Both SELF‑RAG (E4) and the future work in RAG (E9) suggest benefits from joint training. A BART‑style denoising task that corrupts both text and retrieval context could teach the LM to rely on retrieved passages while learning retrieval representations. *Test:* Pre‑train a unified LM‑retriever model on a large corpus (e.g., Wikipedia + BookCorpus) with masked‑span and masked‑retrieval‑passage objectives; evaluate on open‑domain QA (NaturalQuestions) and fact‑verification (FEVER) using EM and F1, comparing to separately pre‑trained RAG baselines. [based on E4, E9]
- **Continual Index Refresh with Retrieval‑Aware Knowledge Distillation**: Performance variance due to stale Wikipedia dumps (E11) suggests a need for mechanisms that adapt to new knowledge without full retraining. Distilling knowledge from a fresh index into the existing retriever could maintain performance while keeping the model lightweight. *Test:* Create two Wikipedia snapshots (2022 vs 2024); train a base retriever on the older snapshot, then apply knowledge‑distillation updates using the newer snapshot. Measure QA accuracy drift on a fixed test set before and after update, reporting ΔEM. [based on E11, E8]
- **Critique‑Guided Retrieval Scheduling**: SELF‑RAG’s critique tokens (E3) indicate that self‑assessment can trigger further retrieval. Systematically studying how critique confidence thresholds affect retrieval frequency could optimize the trade‑off between latency and factuality. *Test:* Implement a variant of SELF‑RAG where critique confidence thresholds are varied (e.g., 0.5, 0.7, 0.9); evaluate on long‑form generation tasks (e.g., WikiHow) measuring factuality (FactCC) and latency (seconds per token). [based on E3, E5]

## rq04: How could HyDE be adapted to a domain-specific search problem such as legal documents?
Papers: hyde

**Evidence**
- E1. HyDE performs search in a document-only embedding space that captures document‑document similarity using an unsupervised contrastive encoder shared by all incoming document corpora. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.3)
- E2. The query vector is obtained by mapping the query to a "hypothetical" document with an instruction‑following language model (InstructLM) and then encoding that document with the same unsupervised contrastive encoder. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.3)
- E3. HyDE assumes the distribution of the generated query embeddings is uni‑modal, i.e., the query is not ambiguous, and leaves the study of ambiguous queries and diversity to future work. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.4)
- E4. HyDE estimates the expected query embedding by sampling N documents from the instruction‑following model and averaging their encoder representations, optionally including the original query embedding. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.4)
- E5. The authors claim HyDE offers performance comparable to a fine‑tuned model at the very beginning of a search system’s life, before supervised dense retrievers become available. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.7)
- E6. HyDE significantly outperforms the state‑of‑the‑art unsupervised dense retriever Contriever and shows strong performance comparable to fine‑tuned retrievers across various tasks (web search, QA, fact verification) and languages (Swedish, Korean, Japanese). (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.1)
- E7. HyDE remains competitive with fine‑tuned models on TREC DL19/20 web‑search benchmarks, achieving comparable MAP and nDCG@10 to ContrieverFT and similar recall@1k, though it lags behind ANCE on nDCG@10. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.5)
- E8. The paper studies building effective dense retrieval systems without relevance labels and without assuming access to test‑time corpora for training, a more realistic setup that avoids over‑engineering on test data. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.3)
- E9. HyDE’s approach relies on an instruction‑following LLM to generate hypothetical documents, which can be customized via the textual instruction (INST) supplied to the model. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.3)
- E10. The unsupervised contrastive encoder used by HyDE is learned from document‑only data and does not require any relevance labels for training. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.3)
- E11. The authors note that HyDE may not be useful over the entire lifespan of a search system; as supervised dense retrievers improve, they will handle more queries while HyDE handles only less common or emerging ones. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.7)
- E12. Future work is identified for handling ambiguous queries and improving diversity in the generated query embeddings. (Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels, p.4)

**Analysis (inferred)**
- HyDE’s reliance on a single instruction‑following LLM to generate hypothetical documents may limit its ability to capture the specialized terminology and citation patterns typical of legal texts. [based on E2, E9; high]
- The assumption of a unimodal query embedding distribution (E3) could be especially problematic for legal queries, which often encode multiple sub‑questions (e.g., facts, statutes, precedents). [based on E3; medium]
- Because HyDE’s contrastive encoder is trained only on document‑only data without relevance signals (E10), it may not learn the nuanced relevance judgments that legal practitioners apply (e.g., binding vs. persuasive authority). [based on E10; medium]
- The averaging of N sampled hypothetical documents (E4) may dilute rare but critical legal concepts, reducing recall for niche statutes or case law. [based on E4; low]
- HyDE performs well in zero‑shot settings across diverse domains (E6, E7), suggesting that with domain‑specific prompt engineering it could achieve competitive retrieval for legal corpora without supervised fine‑tuning. [based on E6, E7, E9; high]

**Proposed directions (hypotheses)**
- **Legal‑Prompted HyDE (LP‑HyDE)**: Customizing the instruction (INST) to include legal‑specific cues (e.g., "generate a brief legal memorandum addressing the query") may steer the LLM to produce hypothetical documents that embed domain terminology and citation style, improving query embedding quality for legal search. *Test:* Dataset: COLIEE 2023 legal case retrieval; Baseline: standard HyDE with generic prompt; Metric: nDCG@10 and recall@1k; Compare LP‑HyDE vs. baseline. [based on E2, E9]
- **Multi‑Modal Query Embedding for Ambiguous Legal Queries**: Legal queries often contain multiple intents (e.g., factual background + statutory interpretation). Extending HyDE to generate a set of diverse hypothetical documents (instead of averaging) can capture a multimodal embedding space, addressing the limitation noted in E3/E12. *Test:* Dataset: LegalBench QA with multi‑intent queries; Baseline: original HyDE (averaged embedding); Metric: MAP and query‑intent coverage (precision@5 for each intent). [based on E3, E4, E12]
- **Contrastive Encoder Fine‑Tuned on Legal Citation Networks**: While HyDE’s encoder is unsupervised (E10), injecting weak supervision from citation graphs (e.g., case‑to‑case citations) can teach the encoder the hierarchical relevance patterns of law without full relevance labels. *Test:* Dataset: Caselaw Access Project; Weak supervision: citation adjacency; Baseline: vanilla HyDE encoder; Metric: citation‑aware recall@1k and nDCG@10. [based on E10, E11]
- **Dynamic Sampling Count for Rare Legal Concepts**: Increasing N for queries containing rare legal terms may prevent dilution of critical signals (E4). An adaptive sampler that raises N when term frequency is low could improve recall for niche statutes. *Test:* Dataset: Statute retrieval benchmark with long‑tail terms; Baseline: fixed N=5; Adaptive N (1–20 based on term rarity); Metric: recall@100 for rare vs. common queries. [based on E4]

## rq05: What new research directions could combine ideas from these papers?
Papers: retro, rag

**Evidence**
- E1. It may be fruitful to investigate if the two components can be jointly pre‑trained from scratch, either with a denoising objective similar to BART or some another objective. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.9)
- E2. The retrieval index can be hot‑swapped to update the model without requiring any retraining. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.9)
- E3. RAG could be employed in a wide variety of scenarios with direct benefit to society, for example by endowing it with a medical index and asking it open‑domain questions on that topic. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.10)
- E4. Our work opens up new avenues for improving language models through explicit memory at unprecedented scale. (Improving language models by retrieving from trillions of tokens, p.1)
- E5. Retro combines a frozen BERT retriever, a differentiable encoder and a chunked cross‑attention mechanism to predict tokens based on an order of magnitude more data than what is typically consumed during training. (Improving language models by retrieving from trillions of tokens, p.1)
- E6. RAG techniques may work well in retrieve‑and‑edit settings and could represent promising future work. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.9)
- E7. Hybrid models that combine parametric memory with non‑parametric (i.e., retrieval‑based) memories can address issues such as difficulty expanding or revising memory, lack of interpretability, and hallucinations. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.1)
- E8. Our RAG models obtain state‑of‑the‑art results on open‑domain QA and generate responses that are more factual, specific, and diverse than a BART baseline. (Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, p.2)
- E9. Retro can be rapidly retrofitted onto pre‑trained transformers with retrieval and still achieve good performance. (Improving language models by retrieving from trillions of tokens, p.1)

**Analysis (inferred)**
- Joint pre‑training of the generator and retriever could yield tighter integration than current two‑stage training, but it is unclear how to balance denoising and retrieval objectives. [based on E1; medium]
- Hot‑swapping the retrieval index enables continual updates without retraining, yet the impact on downstream factuality and latency has not been measured. [based on E2; high]
- Applying RAG to domain‑specific indexes (e.g., medical) promises societal benefit, but domain shift may degrade retrieval quality and require specialized retrievers. [based on E3; medium]
- Scaling explicit memory to trillions of tokens (Retro) shows feasibility, but the trade‑off between memory size and inference speed remains open. [based on E4, E5; high]
- Hybrid parametric‑non‑parametric models address hallucinations, yet the best way to combine them (fusion layer, gating, edit‑based) is still ambiguous. [based on E7; medium]

**Proposed directions (hypotheses)**
- **Joint Denoising‑Retrieval Pre‑training**: Combine a BART‑style denoising loss with a retrieval‑augmented token prediction loss to train generator and retriever from scratch, testing whether tighter coupling improves factuality and reduces reliance on post‑hoc fine‑tuning. *Test:* Fine‑tune on NaturalQuestions open‑domain QA; compare joint‑pre‑trained RAG vs. sequentially trained baseline using Exact Match and F1; report training stability and compute cost. [based on E1, E7]
- **Continual Index Updating for Domain‑Specific RAG**: Leverage hot‑swappable indexes to continuously ingest new medical literature, evaluating whether frequent updates improve answer accuracy without retraining the model. *Test:* Create a rolling medical corpus (e.g., PubMed updates); evaluate on a medical QA benchmark (MedQA) after each weekly index refresh; measure EM, latency, and catastrophic forgetting. [based on E2, E3]
- **Trillion‑Token Memory with Retrieval‑Edit Fusion**: Integrate Retro's trillion‑token retrieval with a retrieve‑and‑edit generation pipeline to see if explicit editing reduces hallucinations while preserving the benefits of massive memory. *Test:* Use the Wiki-40B corpus for retrieval; edit generated answers on open‑domain QA (TriviaQA); compare to standard RAG and Retro using BLEU, factuality score, and edit distance. [based on E5, E6]
- **Efficiency Trade‑off Study for Scalable Explicit Memory**: Systematically benchmark how increasing the size of the retrieval corpus (from billions to trillions of tokens) affects inference latency, memory footprint, and downstream performance, addressing the open scalability question. *Test:* Run Retro on three corpus sizes (1B, 100B, 1T tokens) using the same transformer backbone; evaluate on LAMBADA language modeling and open‑domain QA; report throughput (tokens/s) and accuracy metrics. [based on E4, E5, E9]

## rq06: What experiments could validate an improvement to Fusion-in-Decoder?
Papers: fid

**Evidence**
- E1. Fusion-in-Decoder outperforms existing work on the NaturalQuestions and TriviaQA benchmarks, showing that scaling to a large number of passages and processing them jointly improves accuracy. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.4)
- E2. On NaturalQuestions, the closed‑book T5 model obtains 36.6% accuracy with 11B parameters, while Fusion-in-Decoder obtains 44.1% with 770M parameters plus Wikipedia with BM25 retrieval. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.4)
- E3. The authors evaluate Fusion-in-Decoder on NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA, and SQuAD using the same settings as Lee et al. (2019) and report results on validation sets treated as test sets. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.3, 4)
- E4. Performance of Fusion-in-Decoder (base) improves as the number of retrieved passages increases, as shown in Figure 3 for NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA, and SQuAD. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.4)
- E5. The retrieval component uses either BM25 (bag‑of‑words) or DPR (dense embeddings) to retrieve support passages, allowing comparison of different retrieval methods. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.3)
- E6. BM25 retrieval is implemented with Apache Lucene using default parameters and SpaCy tokenization; DPR uses BERT encoders and FAISS for approximate nearest‑neighbor search. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.3)
- E7. The authors use Wikipedia dumps from Dec 20 2018 for NaturalQuestions and TriviaQA and from Dec 21 2016 for SQuAD, and preprocess passages to 100‑word non‑overlapping chunks. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.4)
- E8. The method processes each retrieved passage with a separate encoder and concatenates the encoder outputs before feeding them to a decoder that generates the answer. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.2)
- E9. The authors note that generative models perform well when evidence from multiple passages needs to be aggregated, compared to extractive approaches. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.4, 2)
- E10. Future work includes making the model more efficient when scaling to a large number of support passages and integrating retrieval into the model for end‑to‑end learning. (Leveraging Passage Retrieval with Generative Models for Open Domain Question Answering, p.5)
